**Optimizing Fiber Photometry Analysis for Acetylcholine Signals in
Feeding Behavior: A Methodological Report**\
1. Foundational Principles for Analyzing Acetylcholine Fiber Photometry
Data\
The analysis of fiber photometry data, particularly for dynamic
neurotransmitters like acetylcholine (ACh), requires meticulous
attention to preprocessing and normalization to ensure data integrity
and valid biological interpretation. These foundational steps are
critical for distinguishing true neural signals from various artifacts
inherent in the recording technique.\
**1.1. Signal Preprocessing: Essential Steps for Data Integrity**\
Effective preprocessing is the first line of defense against data
contamination. Key steps include correcting for motion artifacts,
addressing photobleaching and baseline drift, and applying appropriate
signal filtering.\
**1.1.1. Motion Artifact Correction**\
Fiber photometry signals are notoriously susceptible to artifacts caused
by the animal\'s movement. These artifacts can arise from physical
displacement of the fiber optic relative to the tissue, changes in light
coupling, or even movement-related hemodynamic changes, all of which can
generate fluorescence fluctuations that mimic or obscure genuine neural
signals.^1^ Therefore, robust motion artifact correction is
indispensable.\
A standard and effective approach involves the use of an isosbestic
control channel. This typically involves exciting the fluorescent sensor
at a wavelength where its emission is largely independent of the analyte
of interest (e.g., Ca\<sup\>2+\</sup\> or, in this context, ACh
binding). For GCaMP-like sensors, a wavelength around 405-415 nm often
serves this purpose.^2^ The data provided from the Tucker Davis
Technologies (TDT) system, as indicated by the StoresListing.txt files
within the fpExplorer example data, includes a \"405A\" channel
alongside the \"465A\" signal channel, confirming the use of an
isosbestic control.^4^ This 405 nm channel is designed to capture
fluorescence changes due to motion, photobleaching, and fiber bending,
but ideally not those due to changes in ACh concentration.\
The core of isosbestic correction lies in mathematically relating the
control channel\'s signal to the artifactual component within the signal
channel and then removing it. Several fitting methods exist:
 
-   Polynomial Fitting (as implemented in fpExplorer): The fpExplorer
    software provides \"Standard Polynomial Fitting\" and \"Modified
    Polynomial Fitting\" options.^4^ These methods are likely
    implemented in functions such as normalize_pMat (inspired by the
    pMAT analysis tool) and normalize_dff found within
    fpExplorer_functions.py.^4^ The principle involves fitting the 405
    nm control channel data to the 465 nm signal channel data using a
    polynomial equation. The resulting scaled control channel signal,
    representing the estimated artifact, is then used to correct the
    signal channel. The \"Modified Polynomial Fitting,\" attributed to
    Patrick Mulholland ^4^, suggests an enhancement to a basic
    polynomial approach, though its specific algorithm is not detailed
    in the provided materials.
 
-   Linear Regression (Jing et al., 2020): The reference paper by Jing
    et al. employs a straightforward linear regression to scale the 405
    nm channel signal to best fit the 465 nm signal. This scaled control
    signal is subsequently subtracted from the 465 nm signal to yield a
    corrected trace.^5^ This is equivalent to a 1st-degree polynomial
    fit.
 
-   Robust Regression (e.g., Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares -
    IRLS): An important consideration is how the fitting process handles
    true neural signals that might co-occur with movement. Standard
    Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression, which often underlies basic
    linear or polynomial fits, can be problematic. If a genuine ACh
    transient in the 465 nm channel occurs simultaneously with a
    movement (and thus an artifact in both channels), OLS might
    misinterpret the neural component as part of the shared artifact.
    This can lead to the control channel being \"over-fitted\" to the
    neural event, resulting in the subtraction and attenuation of the
    true biological signal. Recent literature strongly advocates for
    robust regression methods like IRLS to address this.^2^ IRLS
    iteratively assigns lower weights to data points in the signal
    channel that are poorly predicted by the control channel (i.e., the
    unique neural transients). This focuses the fit on the shared
    artifactual components, providing a more accurate estimation of the
    motion artifact and better preservation of the neural signal.^2^
    Tukey\'s bisquare weighting function is often recommended for IRLS
    in this context.^2^\
    The choice of fitting algorithm for isosbestic correction is
    therefore critical for accuracy, particularly if ACh signals and
    animal movement are temporally correlated during behaviors such as
    feeding. If feeding behaviors involve stereotyped movements that are
    temporally locked to the ACh signals of interest, fpExplorer\'s
    current polynomial fitting methods, if OLS-based, might lead to an
    underestimation of the true magnitude of these ACh transients. The
    \"Modified Polynomial Fitting\" within fpExplorer may be an attempt
    to improve upon this, but its specific implementation warrants
    investigation. For maximal accuracy, especially in challenging
    datasets, implementing or utilizing an IRLS-based correction for
    this step should be considered. The work by Seltzer & Barker
    provides a strong rationale and detailed methodology for applying
    IRLS in fiber photometry.^2^\
    **1.1.2. Photobleaching and Baseline Drift Correction**\
    Beyond acute motion artifacts, fiber photometry signals are also
    affected by slower changes. Photobleaching refers to the
    irreversible photochemical destruction of the fluorophore under
    continuous excitation, leading to a gradual decline in overall
    fluorescence intensity. Baseline drift can also occur due to slow
    physiological changes or minor instabilities in the recording
    equipment.^1^\
    Correction strategies include:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Isosbestic Channel Correction: As the control channel is also
    subject to photobleaching and slow drifts, fitting methods that
    align the control channel to the signal channel (discussed in 1.1.1)
    inherently help to correct for these shared slow trends.^2^ This is
    often the primary mechanism for addressing photobleaching.
 
-   Mathematical Detrending/Fitting:
 
    -   Jing et al. (2020) report using the 'BEADS' MATLAB script with a
        very low cut-off frequency (0.0005 cycles/sample) to remove slow
        drift from their signals.^5^ This implies a form of high-pass
        filtering or very slow trend removal.
 
    -   The polynomial fitting routines within fpExplorer ^4^, used for
        normalization, also serve as a detrending method by fitting a
        polynomial curve to the baseline of the signal.
 
    -   Explicit exponential fitting, as described by Zhang et al. ^7^,
        involves modeling the photobleaching decay with a function like
        a×ebx+c and subtracting this fitted decay curve from the raw
        signal.\
        While fitting the isosbestic channel is a powerful tool for
        correcting photobleaching, the mathematical form of this fit
        (e.g., linear, polynomial order) or any additional detrending
        applied directly to the signal channel will influence the
        accuracy of the estimated baseline fluorescence (F0).
        Photobleaching is often a non-linear process. If the chosen
        fitting method does not adequately capture this non-linearity,
        the estimated F0 will be inaccurate across the recording, which
        in turn distorts any dF/F calculations. Therefore, visual
        inspection of the fitted baseline against the
        (control-channel-corrected) signal is crucial to ensure the
        bleaching trend is appropriately modeled.\
        **1.1.3. Filtering Strategies**\
        Signal filtering is employed to remove high-frequency noise
        (e.g., from electronics, or very rapid minor movements not fully
        captured by the isosbestic correction) without distorting the
        physiological signal of interest.^2^\
        Common methods include:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Low-pass filtering: This attenuates frequencies above a specified
    cutoff. Jing et al. (2020) used a 6 Hz low-pass filter for one
    system and a 20 Hz low-pass filter for demodulated TDT signals.^52^
    also recommends low-pass filtering as a standard preprocessing step.
 
-   Moving average/median filter: fpExplorer provides a smoothing filter
    option, defined by filter_window in its settings (defaulting to 10
    samples).^4^ This is likely a moving average filter, where the
    window size dictates the extent of smoothing.\
    The selection of filter parameters (e.g., cutoff frequency, window
    size) is critical and must be made in consideration of the ACh
    sensor\'s response kinetics and the data\'s sampling rate. The
    ACh3.0 sensor exhibits sub-second response kinetics ^5^, meaning it
    can report relatively fast physiological changes in ACh. Overly
    aggressive filtering (e.g., a very low cutoff frequency or an
    excessively wide smoothing window) can attenuate the amplitude and
    slow the perceived kinetics of these true, rapid ACh transients.
    Conversely, insufficient filtering may leave high-frequency noise
    that could obscure subtle signals or interfere with subsequent
    analyses like peak detection. The default smoothing window of 10
    samples in fpExplorer needs to be interpreted relative to the
    data\'s sampling rate after any downsampling steps; for instance, at
    100 Hz sampling, a 10-sample window corresponds to 100 ms of
    smoothing. It is generally advisable to apply the minimum filtering
    necessary and to visually inspect the impact of different filter
    settings on representative data segments.\
    **1.2. Signal Normalization: dF/F vs. Z-score**\
    After preprocessing to remove artifacts and noise, the fluorescence
    signal is typically normalized. Normalization converts the signal
    into standardized units, facilitating comparisons across different
    time points, trials, animals, or experimental conditions. The two
    most common methods are dF/F (delta F over F) and Z-score.\
    **1.2.1. dF/F (Delta F over F)**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Principle: dF/F represents the fractional change in fluorescence
    (ΔF) relative to a baseline fluorescence level (F0). It is
    calculated as ΔF/F = (F - F0) / F0, where F is the fluorescence at a
    given time point and F0 is the baseline fluorescence. This value is
    often multiplied by 100 to be expressed as a percentage.^7^
 
-   F0 Estimation -- The Crucial Step: The accuracy of dF/F values is
    highly dependent on the method used to estimate F0.
 
    -   Jing et al. (2020) Method: F0 is determined by fitting the
        autofluorescence-subtracted data with a 2nd order exponential
        function.^5^ This implies a time-varying F0 that accounts for
        slow drifts like photobleaching.
 
    -   fpExplorer\'s Polynomial Fitting: The \"Standard Polynomial
        Fitting\" and \"Modified Polynomial Fitting\" options in
        fpExplorer are central to its F0 estimation.^4^ After motion
        correction (which involves fitting the control channel to the
        signal channel), a polynomial is likely fitted to the corrected
        signal channel to serve as the time-varying F0. Functions like
        normalize_dff and normalize_pMat in fpExplorer_functions.py
        handle these calculations.^4^
 
    -   Fitted Isosbestic Signal as F0: A robust and widely recommended
        approach is to use the fitted control channel signal (after it
        has been scaled to match the baseline of the signal channel) as
        F0.^2^ The dF/F calculation then becomes (Signal_Corrected -
        Fitted_Control) / Fitted_Control or (Signal_Raw -
        Fitted_Control) / Fitted_Control. This method inherently
        incorporates corrections for motion and photobleaching captured
        by the control channel into the F0 definition.
 
    -   Other Methods: Simpler methods include using a moving average or
        median of a segment of the signal (as potentially used in one of
        the user\'s MATLAB scripts, suggested by the mention of
        \"movmean\" ^12^), or using the median fluorescence of the
        entire recording session ^7^, though the latter is less suitable
        for capturing dynamic changes in F0 due to photobleaching.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Advantages: dF/F can preserve information about the relative
    magnitude of fluorescence changes. It is often considered more
    appropriate than Z-scoring for analyzing spontaneous neural activity
    or when comparing absolute signal changes, as it is less influenced
    by alterations in baseline signal variance.^13^
 
-   Limitations: The method is highly sensitive to the accuracy of F0
    estimation. An incorrectly estimated or poorly fitted F0 will
    systematically scale all dF/F values, potentially leading to
    erroneous interpretations.\
    **1.2.2. Z-score**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Principle: The Z-score transforms the signal by subtracting the mean
    of a defined baseline period and then dividing by the standard
    deviation (SD) of that same baseline period: Z = (F -
    mean(F_baseline)) / SD(F_baseline).^9^ The fluorescence signal (F)
    used here is typically the trace that has already undergone
    preprocessing, including motion correction and potentially an
    initial dF/F-like normalization (e.g., if F0 is a fitted
    polynomial).
 
-   Baseline Selection: The choice of the baseline period is critical
    and significantly impacts the resulting Z-score values. Common
    choices include:
 
    -   A pre-event window: a segment of several seconds immediately
        preceding a stimulus or behavioral onset.
 
    -   A period of known behavioral quiescence: for example, NREM sleep
        was used by Jing et al. (2020) for some of their Z-score
        normalizations.^5^
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   fpExplorer Implementation: fpExplorer provides Z-score calculation
    capabilities.^4^ The batch-z-score-options image in the fpExplorer
    README ^4^ indicates that users can define a baseline window (e.g.,
    \"baseline window start/end\") for Z-score calculation during batch
    processing. This suggests that Z-scoring in fpExplorer is performed
    on a trace that has likely already undergone some form of baseline
    correction or normalization via the polynomial fitting methods.
 
-   Advantages: Z-scoring is useful for standardizing signals across
    different recording sessions, animals, or even varying sensor
    expression levels, as it expresses signal changes in units of
    baseline variability. It is a commonly employed method for
    event-related analyses to assess the statistical \"significance\" of
    a response relative to baseline fluctuations.^13^
 
-   Limitations and Critiques:
 
    -   Sensitivity to Baseline SD: If the baseline SD is very small
        (e.g., due to very low neural activity or excessive signal
        smoothing), Z-scores can become artificially inflated, making
        small actual changes appear very large.
 
    -   Masking True Effects: Z-scoring can obscure true changes in the
        absolute magnitude or kinetics of signals, particularly if the
        baseline characteristics (mean or SD) themselves change between
        conditions or over time (e.g., in longitudinal studies). The
        bioRxiv paper by Hart et al. provides a strong critique of
        Z-scoring for analyzing *spontaneous* neurotransmitter signals,
        arguing it can \"remove variation of experimental
        interest\".^13^
 
    -   Not Universally Applicable: Z-scoring is less suitable for
        analyzing tonic (slow, sustained) shifts in neural activity or
        situations where the baseline itself is dynamic and of
        physiological interest.\
        **1.2.3. Contextual Appropriateness and Recommendations**\
        The decision between dF/F and Z-score normalization is not
        absolute but depends heavily on the specific research question,
        the expected characteristics of the ACh signals (e.g., phasic
        bursts vs. tonic shifts, event-locked vs. spontaneous activity),
        and the properties of the baseline recording period.\
        Jing et al. (2020) exemplify this context-dependent choice by
        using both dF/F0 (calculated from a 2nd order exponential fit
        for F0) for general signal representation and Z-scores
        (normalized to NREM sleep SD) for specific analyses, such as
        those related to sleep-wake cycles.^5^ This demonstrates that
        different normalization strategies can be appropriate even
        within a single comprehensive study. The strong critiques of
        Z-scoring found in the literature, particularly from Hart et al.
        ^13^, are primarily focused on its application to *spontaneous*
        neurotransmitter release and *longitudinal studies* where
        pharmacological manipulations or other interventions might alter
        baseline signal kinetics or variability. In such cases,
        Z-scoring can indeed mask these important changes or
        artificially amplify noise.\
        fpExplorer provides options for both dF/F (via its polynomial
        fitting normalization routines) and Z-score calculations ^4^,
        rightly placing the decision with the user based on their
        analytical goals.\
        For the primary objective of this research---analyzing ACh
        signals during discrete feeding events---which are likely to be
        phasic and time-locked to specific behavioral actions, Z-scoring
        relative to a stable pre-event baseline is a widely accepted and
        justifiable method. This approach allows for the comparison of
        response magnitudes across different trials or animals by
        standardizing the signal changes to the variability observed
        immediately before the event. fpExplorer\'s Z-score
        functionality, when configured with an appropriate pre-event
        baseline window (e.g., -10 seconds to -2 seconds before food
        presentation or the first bite), should be suitable for this
        purpose.\
        However, for more exploratory analyses, such as investigating
        \"everything else like social interaction and stuff,\" or if
        there is a hypothesis that tonic ACh levels change around
        feeding (e.g., due to satiety or anticipation), or if the
        baseline ACh activity itself is expected to vary significantly
        between conditions, then dF/F with a robustly estimated F0 would
        be more appropriate. dF/F better preserves information about
        absolute changes in signal magnitude. In fpExplorer, this would
        involve using the output of the polynomial fitting normalization
        (ideally the \"Modified Polynomial Fitting\" if it proves to be
        more robust, or after an IRLS correction if implemented
        separately).\
        Recommendations:
 
1.  Primary Event-Related Analysis (Feeding Events): Utilize Z-scores.
    Define a consistent pre-event baseline window (e.g., 5-10 seconds
    immediately preceding food presentation or the first interaction,
    ensuring this period is free of other major behavioral events or
    significant signal fluctuations). fpExplorer\'s batch Z-score
    options should facilitate this.
 
2.  Exploratory Analysis or Investigation of Tonic Changes: Employ dF/F.
    Ensure that the F0 used for this calculation is derived from the
    motion-corrected signal trace using fpExplorer\'s polynomial fitting
    routines. It is crucial to visually inspect the F0 fit to confirm it
    accurately captures the baseline trend without being unduly
    influenced by large, transient neural signals.
 
3.  Transparency in Reporting: Regardless of the method chosen for a
    particular analysis, meticulously document the F0 estimation
    technique if using dF/F (e.g., \"F0 was determined by a X-order
    polynomial fit to the 405nm-corrected 465nm signal\") or the exact
    baseline window definition and period if using Z-scores (e.g.,
    \"Z-scores were calculated relative to the mean and standard
    deviation of the signal from -10s to -2s prior to event onset\").
    This level of detail is essential for reproducibility and clear
    interpretation of the findings.\
    **2. Analyzing Event-Related Cholinergic Activity During Feeding
    (and other behaviors)**\
    This section focuses on the methodologies for analyzing fiber
    photometry signals that are temporally locked to specific behavioral
    events, with a primary emphasis on feeding, but also extending to
    grooming and social interaction as per the user\'s interest.\
    **2.1. Defining and Time-Locking Behavioral Events**\
    The accuracy of event-related analysis fundamentally depends on the
    precise and consistent definition and timestamping of behavioral
    events. The user\'s current practice of \"scribing videos that go
    along with the videos like maybe a timestamp and then we show
    grooming and another timestamp food introduced another timestamp
    feeding etc.\" represents a standard and often necessary approach
    for complex, freely moving behaviors.
 
-   Precision and Consistency: The reliability of associating neural
    signals with behavior is directly proportional to the accuracy of
    the behavioral timestamps. Vague definitions or inconsistent marking
    of event onsets and offsets will lead to smeared peri-event averages
    and potentially misleading conclusions.
 
-   fpExplorer\'s Event Handling: fpExplorer is designed to accommodate
    event-based experimental data. It supports event codes from TDT
    systems (e.g., \"Note 1\", \"PrtA 253\" as seen in example data ^4^)
    and can also import events from user-generated CSV files.^4^ This
    flexibility is crucial for integrating manually scribed behavioral
    annotations. The PreviewEventBasedWidget within fpExplorer.py is
    specifically tailored for such analyses.^4^ It is important to
    ensure that the format of the scribed timestamps (likely a CSV file
    with columns for timestamp and event name/type) is compatible with
    fpExplorer\'s import requirements.
 
-   Guidance from Jing et al. (2020): While the specific methods for
    annotating feeding behavior are not detailed in the provided summary
    of Jing et al. ^5^, their work on other paradigms (e.g., using TTL
    pulses for foot-shock synchronization, or computer triggers for
    treadmill experiments) highlights the general importance of precise
    temporal alignment between behavioral events and neural recordings.\
    For complex behaviors like feeding, it is highly beneficial to
    dissect the behavior into distinct, operationally defined
    sub-components rather than using a single, broad \"feeding\" event.
    Feeding is not a monolithic action; it encompasses an appetitive
    phase (e.g., approach to food, investigation), a consummatory phase
    (e.g., biting, chewing, licking), and potentially a
    post-consummatory or satiety phase. ACh is implicated in diverse
    cognitive and motivational processes, including attention, learning,
    and reward processing, any of which could be differentially engaged
    during these distinct sub-phases of feeding. A single \"feeding\"
    timestamp might average out or obscure specific ACh signals related
    to these individual actions (e.g., the decision to initiate feeding,
    the sensory experience of the first bite, or the motor patterns of
    chewing).\
    The user\'s video scribing methodology is well-suited to capture
    this granularity. For instance, distinct timestamps could be
    generated for events such as:\
    \* \"Food Introduced\" (FI)\
    \* \"Approach Initiated\" (AI)\
    \* \"First Sniff/Contact with Food\" (FS)\
    \* \"First Bite\" (FB)\
    \* \"Sustained Chewing Bout Start\" (CS)\
    \* \"Sustained Chewing Bout End\" (CE)\
    \* \"End of Feeding Episode\" (EF)\
    Analyzing ACh signals aligned to these more granular sub-events can
    provide a much richer and more nuanced understanding of cholinergic
    involvement across the entire sequence of feeding behavior.
    fpExplorer\'s event system, with its ability to import events from
    CSV files, should be capable of handling multiple distinct event
    markers corresponding to these sub-components.\
    Recommendation: Develop a detailed ethogram for feeding behavior, as
    well as for grooming and social interaction, with precise,
    unambiguous operational definitions for the onset and offset of each
    behavioral component and sub-component to be analyzed. If multiple
    individuals are involved in scoring, inter-rater reliability should
    be assessed. Structure the scribed data output (likely a CSV) such
    that each defined sub-event has its own unique timestamp and a
    descriptive event label that fpExplorer can recognize.\
    **2.2. Peri-Event Signal Analysis: Visualization and Initial
    Assessment**\
    Once behavioral events are accurately timestamped and aligned with
    the preprocessed and normalized photometry trace, peri-event
    analysis can be performed to visualize and assess average neural
    responses.

```
-   Peri-Event Time Histograms (PETHs) / Averaged Traces: This is the
    most common visualization method. It involves:
 
    1.  Extracting segments of the normalized photometry signal (dF/F or
        Z-score) centered around each instance of a specific event
        (e.g., all \"First Bite\" events).
 
    2.  Aligning these segments to the event onset (time = 0).
 
    3.  Calculating the average signal across all trials at each time
        point relative to the event.
 
    4.  Plotting this average trace, typically with time on the x-axis
        and the normalized signal on the y-axis. Shading around the mean
        trace usually represents the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) or
        a confidence interval, providing an indication of trial-to-trial
        consistency.^7^ fpExplorer\'s PreviewEventBasedWidget is
        designed to perform such peri-event averaging.^4^

```
-   Heatmaps: Heatmaps offer a complementary view by displaying the
    signal from *every individual trial* for a given event, rather than
    just the average. Typically:
 
    1.  Time relative to event onset is plotted on the x-axis.
 
    2.  Trial number is plotted on the y-axis.
 
    3.  The amplitude of the normalized signal at each time point for
        each trial is represented by color intensity (e.g., red for high
        activity, blue for low activity). Heatmaps are invaluable for
        identifying trial-to-trial variability in the neural response,
        observing learning-related changes across trials, or detecting
        subgroups of responses that might be obscured by simple
        averaging.^7^ While the provided snippets do not explicitly
        state that fpExplorer generates heatmaps, this is a standard
        visualization feature in many fiber photometry analysis packages
        and could be implemented using exported data in MATLAB or Python
        if not directly available in fpExplorer.

-   Baseline Period for Peri-Event Z-scores: If Z-scores are used for
    peri-event analysis, the baseline period (e.g., -10s to -2s relative
    to event onset) must be carefully chosen from the pre-event portion
    of the analysis window. This baseline should ideally reflect a

    **2.3. Quantification Metrics for Cholinergic Transients**\
    Visual inspection of peri-event averages and heatmaps provides
    qualitative insights. For statistical comparison, specific features
    of the ACh response profile must be quantified. The choice of
    metrics should be guided by the observed morphology of the ACh
    signal during the behavior (e.g., is it a sharp peak, a sustained
    elevation, or a rapid burst of activity?) and the specific
    hypotheses being tested.\
    Commonly used quantification metrics include:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Peak Amplitude: The maximum (or minimum, for inhibitory responses)
    value of the normalized signal (dF/F or Z-score) occurring within a
    defined time window after the event onset.^5^
 
-   Latency to Peak: The time elapsed from the event onset (time = 0) to
    the occurrence of the peak amplitude.
 
-   Duration of Response: The length of time for which the signal
    remains significantly elevated (or suppressed) above a defined
    threshold (e.g., 2 or 3 standard deviations from the mean of the
    pre-event baseline).
 
-   Area Under the Curve (AUC): The integral of the normalized signal
    over a defined time window post-event (e.g., from event onset to +10
    seconds). AUC can reflect the total change in ACh levels or the
    overall strength and duration of the neural response. fpExplorer\'s
    PeriEventOptionsWindow includes options for calculating \"AUC-pre\"
    and \"AUC-post,\" indicating this quantification capability.^4^
 
-   Rise Time / Decay Time (Kinetics): The time taken for the signal to
    rise from baseline to its peak (or a certain percentage of the peak,
    e.g., 10-90% rise time), and the time taken for the signal to decay
    from the peak back towards baseline. Jing et al. (2020) mention
    fitting rise and decay phases of fluorescence signals to
    single-exponential functions to obtain time constants
    (τ\<sub\>on\</sub\> and τ\<sub\>off\</sub\>) during sensor
    characterization.^5^
 
-   Frequency of Transients/Spikes: If the ACh signals manifest as
    discrete, rapid \"spike-like\" events rather than, or in addition
    to, slower modulations, their frequency (number of events per unit
    time) within a defined window around the behavioral event can be a
    relevant metric. fpExplorer includes a \"Spike Detection\" feature,
    accessible via the PeakSettingsWindow and
    AdvancedPeakSettingsWindow, which uses parameters such as prominence
    and distance to identify such transients.^4^\
    The nature of the ACh signal (e.g., a phasic burst, a sustained
    elevation, or a brief inhibition) during different phases of feeding
    will dictate the most informative quantification metrics. For
    instance, if feeding onset triggers a sharp, brief increase in ACh,
    then peak amplitude, latency to peak, and perhaps the frequency of
    rapid transients would be key metrics. If, however, feeding is
    associated with a more sustained elevation of ACh throughout the
    consummatory period, then AUC over that consumption window or the
    mean signal amplitude during this period might be more relevant than
    a single peak value. It is advisable to first visually inspect the
    average peri-event traces and heatmaps for each defined feeding
    sub-event to understand the typical ACh signal dynamics. Based on
    these observations, 2-3 key metrics that best capture these dynamics
    should be selected for statistical comparison.\
    **2.4. Statistical Approaches for Event-Related Data**\
    Comparing quantified metrics across different conditions (e.g.,
    feeding vs. baseline, palatable vs. standard chow) or groups
    requires appropriate statistical tests.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Basic Comparisons:
 
    -   Paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests: These are suitable
        for comparing a post-event metric (e.g., peak Z-score, AUC) to a
        pre-event baseline value within the same group of animals.
 
    -   Unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests: Used for comparing
        metrics between two independent groups of animals.
 
    -   ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): For comparing metrics across more
        than two groups or multiple conditions. Repeated measures ANOVA
        is appropriate if comparing multiple conditions within the same
        animals (e.g., response to different types of food). Post-hoc
        tests (e.g., Tukey\'s, Bonferroni) are then used to identify
        specific group/condition differences. Jing et al. (2020) report
        using two-sided Student's t-tests for group differences.^5^
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Advanced Time-Series Analysis: While comparisons of summary metrics
    are common, they inherently discard information about the temporal
    evolution of the neural signal. More advanced statistical approaches
    can analyze the entire peri-event time course.
 
    -   Functional Linear Mixed Models (FLMM): This approach,
        highlighted in recent literature ^15^, treats the entire
        peri-event signal as a continuous function rather than reducing
        it to a single summary statistic. FLMMs can model the signal at
        each time point as a function of experimental variables (e.g.,
        behavioral condition, trial number) while simultaneously
        accounting for trial-to-trial variability and correlations
        between data points within a trial. Crucially, they can
        incorporate random effects to model between-animal differences.
        This allows for hypothesis testing at *each time point* along
        the peri-event window, identifying precisely *when* significant
        differences emerge between conditions. FLMMs generally offer
        increased statistical power and a more nuanced understanding of
        temporal dynamics compared to traditional methods that rely on
        summary statistics.^15^ The eLife paper by PNAS RP95802
        specifically demonstrates that FLMM can reveal significant
        effects obscured by standard analysis approaches.
 
    -   Cluster-based permutation tests: This is another non-parametric
        method suitable for comparing time series data between
        conditions. It helps to address the multiple comparisons problem
        that arises when testing for differences at many time points
        across the peri-event window by identifying clusters of
        temporally contiguous time points where a significant difference
        exists.\
        For producing rigorous and publishable findings, especially if
        the ACh signals exhibit complex temporal dynamics or if the
        effects are subtle, transitioning beyond simple summary
        statistic comparisons to methods like FLMMs is increasingly
        becoming the standard in the field. While fpExplorer itself may
        not directly implement FLMMs (which typically require
        specialized packages in R or Python), the trial-by-trial
        peri-event traces exported from fpExplorer can serve as input
        for such advanced statistical modeling. Investing time in
        learning and applying these methods can significantly enhance
        the quality, interpretability, and impact of the research
        findings. If FLMMs are initially too complex to implement,
        ensure that any summary-statistic comparisons are
        well-justified, that the assumptions of the chosen statistical
        tests are met, and that multiple relevant metrics (e.g., peak,
        AUC, latency) are considered to provide a more complete picture
        of the ACh response.\

        **3.3. \"Industry Standard\" and \"Correctness\" in the Context
        of Fiber Photometry Analysis**\
        The terms \"industry standard\" and \"correctness\" in
        scientific research refer to methodologies that are widely
        adopted, rigorously validated, well-documented in peer-reviewed
        literature, and fundamentally sound in their application of
        scientific principles.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Evolving \"Industry Standards\" in Fiber Photometry: Key elements
    that are increasingly considered standard for high-quality fiber
    photometry analysis include ^1^:
 
    1.  Use of an Isosbestic Control Channel: This is fundamental for
        addressing motion artifacts.
 
    2.  Robust Motion Correction Fitting: Methods that accurately
        separate neural signals from artifacts, with a growing
        recognition that robust regression techniques (like IRLS) can be
        superior to simple OLS fitting when neural activity and motion
        might be correlated.^2^
 
    3.  Careful and Justified Normalization: Transparent reporting and
        appropriate selection of dF/F (with a well-defined F0) or
        Z-score (with a well-defined baseline) based on the specific
        research question and data characteristics.^13^
 
    4.  Appropriate Statistical Analysis: Employing statistical methods
        that account for the data structure (e.g., repeated measures,
        time-series nature, within- and between-subject variability),
        with a trend towards more sophisticated models like FLMMs.^15^
 
    5.  Comprehensive Methodological Reporting: Detailed description of
        all analysis steps, parameters, and software used to ensure
        reproducibility.
 
-   Assessing \"Correctness\":
 
    1.  fpExplorer: The \"correctness\" of fpExplorer\'s analyses hinges
        significantly on the specific algorithms implemented for its
        \"Standard Polynomial Fitting\" and, particularly, its
        \"Modified Polynomial Fitting.\" If the \"Modified\" version
        effectively implements principles of robust regression or
        employs a highly validated F0 estimation strategy, it could
        achieve a high degree of \"correctness.\" The structured nature
        of fpExplorer can also reduce the likelihood of user error in
        applying a consistent pipeline. However, if its core fitting
        routines are based on simple OLS without adequately addressing
        the potential confounding of neural signals with motion
        artifacts, its \"correctness\" might be compromised for datasets
        where such correlations are strong.
 
    2.  MATLAB Scripts: MATLAB offers the *potential* to achieve maximal
        \"correctness\" because any algorithm, including the most
        current and sophisticated ones described in the literature, can
        be implemented. However, the actual \"correctness\" is entirely
        dependent on the user\'s ability to accurately code, validate,
        and apply these methods. A flawed or inappropriately applied
        custom MATLAB script can lead to less \"correct\" results than a
        well-vetted, standardized tool.

**4. Recommendations for Optimizing Your Analysis Pipeline**\
Based on the comparison of tools and current best practices, the
following recommendations are provided to optimize the analysis pipeline
for investigating ACh signals during feeding and other behaviors.\
**4.1. Leveraging fpExplorer\'s Strengths**\
fpExplorer offers several features that can streamline the initial
stages of data handling and analysis ^4^:
 
-   Data Import and Organization: Utilize fpExplorer for the initial
    import of TDT raw data files or your pre-formatted CSV signal files.
    Its ability to handle the TDT folder structure is a significant
    convenience.
 
-   Behavioral Event Synchronization: The capability to import event
    timestamps from CSV files is crucial for aligning your manually
    scribed behavioral annotations (feeding, grooming, social
    interaction) with the photometry data.
 
-   Video Integration: The fpVideoCreator module is a valuable asset.
    Generating video clips synchronized with peri-event photometry data
    will be extremely useful for verifying behavioral scoring,
    presentations, and potentially as supplementary material for
    publications.
 
-   Initial Visualization and Exploration: Use fpExplorer\'s plotting
    capabilities to preview raw signals, the effects of initial
    preprocessing steps (like downsampling or basic filtering), and to
    generate initial peri-event averages. This can help in identifying
    obvious issues or interesting patterns early on.
 
-   Basic Quantification and Batch Processing: If its normalization
    methods are deemed sufficiently robust after further investigation
    (see 4.2), fpExplorer\'s batch processing for calculating Z-scores
    or dF/F, and its tools for AUC or spike detection, can be efficient
    for processing multiple datasets consistently.\
    **4.2. Addressing Potential Gaps with MATLAB or fpExplorer
    Enhancements**\
    While fpExplorer provides a solid foundation, certain critical
    processing steps may benefit from the greater algorithmic control
    offered by MATLAB, or by enhancing fpExplorer itself.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Motion Artifact Correction -- The Primary Concern:
 
    -   The \"Standard Polynomial Fitting\" and \"Modified Polynomial
        Fitting\" in fpExplorer are central to its motion correction.^4^
        It is essential to understand their precise mathematical
        implementation. If these methods are based on Ordinary Least
        Squares (OLS) regression, they may be susceptible to
        inaccuracies if significant neural activity (ACh transients)
        correlates with movement during feeding. As discussed (Insight
        1.1.1.A), OLS can misattribute parts of the neural signal as
        artifact, leading to signal attenuation.^2^
 
    -   Recommendation:
 
        1.  Investigate fpExplorer\'s Fitting: Examine the Python code
            in fpExplorer_functions.py to determine the exact nature of
            the polynomial fitting, particularly the \"Modified\"
            version. If it already incorporates robust regression
            principles (e.g., down-weighting outliers during the fit),
            it may be adequate.
 
        2.  Consider IRLS Implementation: If fpExplorer\'s methods are
            OLS-based, and if visual inspection suggests that
            feeding-related ACh signals are being suppressed or
            distorted by the motion correction, implementing an
            Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) regression of
            the 405nm control signal onto the 465nm signal channel is
            highly recommended. This could be done:
 
            -   In MATLAB: Export the raw 405nm and 465nm data, perform
                IRLS correction in MATLAB, and then import the corrected
                465nm trace (or a dF/F derived from it) back into
                fpExplorer as a CSV for further event-related analysis.
 
            -   As an fpExplorer Enhancement: If feasible and Python
                expertise is available, adding an IRLS fitting option to
                fpExplorer_functions.py would be a significant
                improvement to the tool, making it more robust for a
                wider range of experimental conditions.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   F0 Estimation for dF/F Calculation:
 
    -   The accuracy of dF/F normalization depends critically on the F0
        estimation. If using fpExplorer\'s polynomial fitting, this
        fitted curve (after control channel regression) effectively
        becomes F0.
 
    -   Recommendation: Ensure that the F0 used for dF/F calculation is
        derived from the *motion-corrected* 465nm signal. A robust
        method, as suggested by Lerner et al. and consistent with the
        principles in ^2^ and ^6^, is to use the fitted (scaled) control
        channel signal as F0, such that dF/F = (Signal_Raw -
        Fitted_Control) / Fitted_Control. Verify if fpExplorer\'s dF/F
        calculation aligns with this or a similarly robust principle. If
        F0 is derived, for example, from the raw 465nm signal before
        adequate motion correction, or via a simple moving average that
        doesn\'t account for artifacts, the dF/F values could be
        inaccurate.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Advanced Statistical Analysis:
 
    -   fpExplorer provides tools for quantification but not for
        advanced statistical modeling of time-series data (e.g., FLMMs).
 
    -   Recommendation: For rigorous hypothesis testing, especially when
        comparing temporal dynamics of ACh signals across different
        feeding sub-events or conditions, plan to export the relevant
        data from fpExplorer. This would typically be trial-by-trial
        peri-event traces (either Z-scored or dF/F) or quantified
        metrics (peak, AUC, etc.). These data can then be analyzed using
        specialized statistical packages in R (e.g., for FLMMs using
        packages like refund or lme4/gamm4), Python (e.g., statsmodels,
        pymer4), or MATLAB\'s Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox.
        This approach aligns with best practices for robust inference
        from fiber photometry data.^15^\
        **4.3. Hybrid Approach Workflow Suggestion**\
        A hybrid workflow, combining the strengths of fpExplorer for
        data management and user-friendly initial analyses with the
        algorithmic power of MATLAB (or Python scripting) for critical
        processing steps, is likely to provide the best balance of
        efficiency and scientific rigor.
 
1.  Data Import & Initial Synchronization (fpExplorer):
 
    -   Import raw TDT data into fpExplorer.
 
    -   Import your manually scribed behavioral event timestamps
        (formatted as a CSV compatible with fpExplorer).
 
    -   Use fpVideoCreator to generate synchronized video snippets for
        key behavioral epochs. This helps in verifying event timing and
        for presentations.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
1.  Core Signal Preprocessing (Iterative; fpExplorer and/or MATLAB):
 
    -   Initial Filtering (fpExplorer or MATLAB): Apply a gentle
        low-pass filter (e.g., 15-20 Hz cutoff) to the raw 465nm and
        405nm signals.
 
    -   Motion Artifact Correction & F0 Estimation:
 
        -   Option A (fpExplorer-centric, with scrutiny):

        **5.1. Key Visualization Types**\
        To comprehensively present your findings on ACh dynamics during
        feeding and other behaviors, consider incorporating the
        following types of plots:
 
-   Peri-event Rasters and Heatmaps: These are essential for displaying
    trial-by-trial activity aligned to specific behavioral events (e.g.,
    \"First Bite,\" onset of grooming).
 
    -   Heatmaps: Typically plot time relative to the event on the
        x-axis, trial number on the y-axis, and the normalized signal
        amplitude (dF/F or Z-score) as a color intensity. They are

        trials, identifying trial-to-trial variability, observing
        potential learning effects, or detecting sub-populations of
        responses.^7^
 
    -   Rasters (for discrete events/spikes): If ACh signals manifest as
        discrete \"spikes,\" a raster plot showing the timing of these
        spikes across multiple trials aligned to a behavioral event can
        be informative.

    **6. Guidance on Experimental Design for Acetylcholine and Feeding
    Studies**\
    A robust experimental design is the foundation upon which reliable
    and interpretable fiber photometry results are built. This section
    provides guidance on designing experiments to investigate ACh
    dynamics during feeding, drawing from the cited Jing et al. (2020)
    paper and general best practices in behavioral neuroscience and
    fiber photometry.\
    **6.1. Drawing from Jing et al. (2020) and General Best Practices**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Sensor Selection and Control Considerations:
 
    -   Sensor: The use of an optimized ACh sensor, such as the GACh3.0
        described by Jing et al. ^5^, is appropriate for detecting ACh
        dynamics with good sensitivity and temporal resolution.
 
    -   Control Experiments: While challenging for naturalistic
        behaviors, consider potential control experiments to strengthen
        interpretations. Jing et al. (2020) utilized several controls in
        their broader study, including a ligand-insensitive version of
        the sensor (ACh3.0-mut) and vesicular ACh transporter knockout
        mice (VAChT\<sup\>-/-\</sup\>) to confirm the cholinergic nature
        and synaptic origin of signals.^5^ For behavioral studies, this
        might involve:
 
        -   Expressing a non-ACh-sensitive fluorescent protein (e.g.,
            eYFP) in the same brain region and with the same fiber
            placement in a separate cohort of animals to control for
            non-specific fluorescence changes related to behavior or
            movement that might not be fully captured by the isosbestic
            channel.
 
        -   Pharmacological manipulations (e.g., local administration of
            ACh receptor antagonists) could be used to confirm that
            observed signals are indeed ACh-dependent, though this can
            be complex to implement and interpret in freely moving
            animals during complex behaviors.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Behavioral Paradigms and Annotation:
 
    -   Clear Definition and Granularity of Feeding Epochs: As
        emphasized in Section 2.1, precisely defining and timestamping
        different phases of feeding behavior is critical. Instead of a
        single \"feeding\" event, consider annotating sub-components
        such as:
 
        -   Food presentation/availability.
 
        -   Latency to approach food.
 
        -   First contact/sniff of food.
 
        -   First bite/lick.
 
        -   Duration of individual chewing/licking bouts.
 
        -   Inter-bout intervals.
 
        -   Total consumption time / amount consumed (if measurable).
 
        -   Cessation of feeding. This detailed annotation allows for a
            more nuanced analysis of how ACh signals evolve across the
            entire feeding sequence.
 
    ```{=html}
    <!-- -->
    ```
    -   Control Behaviors: To assess the specificity of feeding-related
        ACh signals, it is essential to record and analyze ACh dynamics
        during other distinct, quantifiable behaviors. The planned
        analysis of grooming and social interaction is good. Also
        include periods of:
 
        -   Quiet wakefulness (animal awake but not actively engaged in
            a specific task).
 
        -   Locomotion (e.g., exploration in the cage, distinct from
            goal-directed approach to food). Comparing ACh signals
            during feeding to these other behaviors will help determine
            if observed changes are specific to feeding or represent
            more general arousal or motor activity.
 
    ```{=html}
    <!-- -->
    ```
    -   Habituation: Ensure animals are thoroughly habituated to the
        experimental environment, the fiber optic tether, and any
        experimental procedures (e.g., food presentation method) before
        data collection begins. This minimizes stress-induced ACh
        fluctuations that could confound the interpretation of
        feeding-related signals. Stress itself can significantly
        modulate feeding behavior and LC-NE activity, which might
        interact with cholinergic systems.^10^
 
    -   Consistency of Food Presentation and Type: If comparing
        responses to different food types (e.g., standard chow vs.
        palatable high-fat/sugar food), ensure that the presentation
        method, novelty, and other contextual factors are as consistent
        as possible, apart from the food itself.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Number of Subjects and Trials:
 
    -   Statistical Power: Adequate statistical power is crucial for
        detecting true effects. While Jing et al. (2020) provide
        examples of sample sizes for *in vitro* or simpler *in vivo*
        preparations (e.g., n=5-7 for some cell culture/slice
        experiments ^5^), behavioral studies in rodents typically
        require larger group sizes to account for inter-animal
        variability. A common range is 8-12+ animals per experimental
        group, but this should ideally be determined by a power analysis
        based on expected effect sizes and variability.
 
    -   Multiple Trials: Collecting data from multiple feeding trials
        (or bouts) per animal is necessary. This allows for assessment
        of the consistency of ACh responses within individual animals
        and provides data for averaging, which can improve the
        signal-to-noise ratio.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Video Recording and Behavioral Scoring:
 
    -   High-Quality, Synchronized Video: Continue to use high-quality
        video recording that is tightly synchronized with the fiber
        photometry data acquisition. This is fundamental for accurate
        post-hoc behavioral annotation.
 
    -   Standardized Scoring: Develop a clear, unambiguous ethogram for
        all behaviors of interest (feeding components, grooming, social
        interactions, etc.). If multiple individuals are involved in
        behavioral scoring, ensure they are trained to use the ethogram
        consistently and assess inter-rater reliability.
 
    -   Automated Behavioral Analysis: If available and validated for
        your specific behaviors and experimental setup, consider using
        automated behavioral analysis software (e.g., DeepLabCut for
        pose estimation, followed by custom scripts for behavior
        classification). This can complement manual scoring, increase
        throughput, and potentially improve objectivity, though it
        requires careful validation.
 
    -   Blinded Scoring: Whenever feasible, behavioral scoring should be
        performed by an observer who is blind to the experimental
        conditions or the corresponding photometry data to minimize
        potential bias.\
        The temporal precision of behavioral annotation is paramount for
        accurately correlating ACh signals with specific phases of
        complex behaviors like feeding. ACh signals can be rapid and
        transient ^5^, and feeding itself is a dynamic process.
        Imprecise timestamps or overly broad behavioral categories can
        smear out or misattribute neural correlates. Therefore,
        investing significant effort in developing a detailed ethogram,
        ensuring consistent application by scorers, and achieving high
        temporal resolution in annotations will be critical for the
        success of the study. The fpVideoCreator tool associated with
        fpExplorer ^4^ can be a valuable aid in linking these precise
        behavioral annotations with the corresponding photometry data
        segments.\

        (IRLS) regression to fit the 405nm control signal to the 465nm
        signal channel. This can be done in MATLAB, with the corrected
        trace imported back into fpExplorer. This aligns with advanced
        best practices for minimizing signal distortion.^2^
