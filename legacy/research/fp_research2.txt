**Analysis of GACh3.0 Fiber Photometry Data: Methods, Best Practices,
and Advanced Techniques**\
I. Introduction to GACh3.0 Fiber Photometry Analysis\
A. Significance of Acetylcholine Monitoring with GACh3.0\
Acetylcholine (ACh) is a paramount neurotransmitter, integral to a vast
array of brain functions, including learning, memory consolidation,
attentional processes, and the regulation of sleep-wake cycles.^1^ The
advent of genetically encoded (GE) sensors, such as GACh3.0, has
revolutionized the study of ACh dynamics, permitting researchers to
monitor its release with unprecedented spatiotemporal precision in
awake, behaving animals.^1^ GACh3.0, an optimized iteration within the
family of G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-based ACh sensors
(GRAB\<sub\>ACh\</sub\>), offers significant improvements in sensitivity
and kinetic properties over earlier versions and alternative detection
methodologies.^3^ A particularly salient feature of GACh3.0 is its
capacity to resolve rapid, transient decreases in ACh levels associated
with specific behavioral or cognitive events, a capability critical for
understanding the nuanced roles of cholinergic signaling.^4^
Consequently, the precise measurement of ACh dynamics using tools like
GACh3.0 is indispensable for dissecting the complex neural circuits that
underpin a wide spectrum of behaviors and for elucidating the
cholinergic dysregulation implicated in various neurological and
psychiatric disorders.^1^\
**B. Overview of the Fiber Photometry Data Analysis Workflow**\
The analysis of data acquired through fiber photometry is a multi-stage
process designed to extract biologically meaningful signals from raw
fluorescence measurements. This workflow typically commences with data
acquisition, where fluorescence signals from the ACh-sensitive sensor
(e.g., excited at \~465-470 nm for green sensors like GACh3.0) and a
control, isosbestic wavelength (typically \~405-415 nm) are
simultaneously recorded.^7^ The subsequent pre-processing steps are
crucial and involve the application of digital filters to remove noise,
correction of artifacts arising from animal movement and photobleaching,
and estimation of the baseline fluorescence (F0).^9^ Following
pre-processing, the signal is normalized, commonly by calculating the
change in fluorescence relative to baseline (ΔF/F) or by converting to a
z-score.^12^ The final stage often involves event-related analysis,
where these normalized signals are aligned to specific behavioral events
or stimuli, or correlated with continuous behavioral readouts to
investigate the relationship between cholinergic activity and
behavior.^7^ Each step in this pipeline is pivotal, as raw fluorescence
data are invariably contaminated by various noise sources,
motion-induced artifacts, and the progressive decay of signal due to
photobleaching, all of which can obscure or distort the underlying
neural signals.^6^\
**C. Critical Need for Robust Methodologies**\
The selection and implementation of analytical methods in fiber
photometry profoundly influence the interpretation of experimental
outcomes. A significant challenge in the field is the lack of
universally standardized analysis techniques, which can lead to
considerable variability in results and hinder direct comparisons
between studies.^17^ Artifacts inherent in fiber photometry
recordings---including system-related noise (e.g., electrical
interference), photobleaching of the sensor and tissue autofluorescence,
movement-related signal fluctuations, and hemodynamic changes---can
easily confound the true neural signals if not meticulously and
appropriately addressed.^11^\
The increasing adoption and accessibility of fiber photometry, combined
with the expanding repertoire of sophisticated genetically encoded
sensors like GACh3.0, underscore an urgent and growing necessity for the
neuroscience community to embrace and implement rigorous, transparent,
and validated data analysis pipelines.^7^ Without a concerted effort
towards methodological standardization and best practices, the field
risks generating findings that may lack reproducibility or are prone to
misinterpretation. This is particularly pertinent given that raw fiber
photometry data, while rich in potential, are susceptible to numerous
confounding factors.^6^ The variability in analysis approaches currently
employed ^17^ means that conclusions drawn from similar experimental
paradigms could differ merely due to analytical choices. Therefore, a
robust understanding and consistent application of best-practice
analysis methods are not merely desirable but are fundamental to
ensuring the validity, reliability, and translational impact of research
utilizing this powerful *in vivo* recording technique.\

**III. Best Practices in GACh3.0 Fiber Photometry Data Processing**\
The successful interpretation of fiber photometry data, particularly
from sensitive neurotransmitter sensors like GACh3.0, hinges on a series
of carefully executed data processing steps. These steps aim to mitigate
artifacts and extract the true underlying biological signal.\
**A. Pre-processing and Initial Signal Conditioning**\
1. Raw Data Inspection and Quality Control\
The initial step in any robust fiber photometry analysis pipeline is a
thorough inspection of the raw data traces from both the signal (e.g.,
\~470 nm excitation for GACh3.0) and the control/isosbestic (e.g., \~405
nm excitation) channels. This visual check is critical for identifying
overt problems such as LED malfunction, intermittent fiber optic cable
disconnections, signal saturation due to excessive LED power, or periods
of unusually high noise.^11^ Any segments of data deemed invalid due to
such technical issues (e.g., logged disconnections or clear signal loss)
should be excluded from further analysis to prevent the introduction of
spurious results.^11^\
Early and meticulous quality control is of paramount importance.
Artifacts that are introduced at the level of the experimental setup or
hardware---for instance, a loosely connected fiber patch cord,
inappropriate LED power settings leading to signal saturation or
excessive photobleaching, or significant light leakage---cannot always
be perfectly rectified through post-hoc computational methods. Such
uncorrected or poorly corrected artifacts can lead to profound
misinterpretations of the data. Therefore, documenting these initial
quality control checks and, where possible, rectifying issues during or
immediately after data acquisition, is significantly more effective than
relying exclusively on subsequent computational correction algorithms.\
**2. Low-Pass Filtering**\
Rationale: Raw fiber photometry signals are often contaminated with
high-frequency noise. This noise can originate from various sources,
including electrical components of the photometry system (e.g., 50/60 Hz
electrical hum), detector noise, or other rapid, non-physiological
fluctuations.^11^ Low-pass filtering is applied to attenuate these
high-frequency components, as the biological signals captured by
neurotransmitter sensors like GACh3.0 typically occur on slower
timescales (sub-second to seconds) relative to this noise.^4^\
Cutoff Selection: The choice of the low-pass filter\'s cutoff frequency
is a critical parameter. It must be selected judiciously, taking into
account the known response kinetics of the GACh3.0 sensor and the
specific characteristics of the noise present in the recording.^11^ For
instance, the GACh2.0 sensor, a precursor to GACh3.0, exhibits response
kinetics in the range of hundreds of milliseconds (e.g., rise time \~200
ms, decay time \~700 ms).^2^ While GACh3.0 is an optimized sensor, its
fundamental response characteristics will still be considerably slower
than very high-frequency noise. Common practice involves using cutoff
frequencies in the range of 1-10 Hz. For example, the GuPPy analysis
toolkit employs a moving average filter, which acts as a low-pass filter
^23^, and other studies have reported specific cutoff values, such as 3
Hz.^26^\
A crucial consideration for GACh3.0 is its documented ability to measure
rapid decreases in ACh levels, for example, within 250 ms following
certain stimuli.^4^ This capability for resolving fast physiological
changes means that the low-pass filter cutoff must be chosen with
particular care. An excessively low cutoff frequency (e.g., below 2 Hz)
risks attenuating or smoothing out these important, rapid cholinergic
dynamics, potentially leading to a loss of critical temporal
information. Conversely, a cutoff set too high may not adequately remove
the contaminating noise. Therefore, an optimal cutoff frequency is one
that effectively preserves the true physiological signal dynamics while
sufficiently attenuating noise, a balance that requires careful
consideration of the sensor\'s kinetic properties and the specific
signal features of interest.\
Filter Type: To prevent the introduction of artificial time lags or
phase distortions into the data, zero-phase filters are strongly
recommended. This can be achieved by applying a filter (e.g., a
Butterworth filter) in both the forward and reverse directions or by
using a centered moving average filter, as is implemented in the GuPPy
software.^23^\
**3. Addressing Photobleaching**\
Nature of Photobleaching: Photobleaching is an unavoidable phenomenon in
fluorescence imaging, characterized by an irreversible decline in the
fluorescence intensity of a fluorophore (and also tissue
autofluorescence) over periods of prolonged or repeated light
exposure.^15^ In fiber photometry, this manifests as a gradual, often
exponential, decay in the recorded signal baseline throughout a
recording session.\
Role of Isosbestic Channel: A primary method for correcting
photobleaching involves the use of the isosbestic control channel. Since
this channel is also subject to photobleaching (as it involves light
delivery and fluorescence detection from the same tissue volume and
optical path), fitting the isosbestic channel\'s signal to the GACh3.0
signal channel and subsequently performing normalization (e.g., ΔF/F
calculation) inherently helps to correct for the photobleaching effects
that are common to both channels.^4^\
Detrending Methods (if no isosbestic channel is available or for
supplementary correction):
 
-   Exponential Fits: The decay due to photobleaching is often
    well-approximated by single or double exponential functions. Fitting
    such functions to the signal trend and then subtracting this fit is
    a common detrending approach.^21^ The GuPPy toolkit, for instance,
    can implement an exponential fit for photobleaching correction if an
    isosbestic channel was not recorded or is not used.^23^ Similarly,
    the FiPhA platform offers options for modeling photobleaching
    effects using linear or exponential models.^12^
 
-   Polynomial Fits: While less commonly used specifically for
    photobleaching, polynomial functions can be employed as part of a
    broader detrending strategy to remove slow drifts from the
    signal.^29^ However, high-order polynomials risk overfitting the
    data.
 
-   Other Baseline Fitting Algorithms: Techniques such as Adaptive
    Iteratively Reweighted Penalized Least Squares (airPLS) or other
    specialized baseline fitting algorithms can be effective for
    removing complex baseline drifts, but they typically require careful
    optimization of multiple parameters.\
    The interplay between isosbestic correction and other detrending
    methods is important. While the isosbestic channel serves as the
    primary means for photobleaching correction, the effectiveness of
    this correction is contingent upon a robust and accurate fit of the
    control channel to the signal channel. In scenarios involving very
    long recording durations or complex bleaching profiles that are not
    perfectly mirrored in the isosbestic channel, it may be necessary to
    apply additional detrending procedures to the *corrected* signal
    (i.e., after isosbestic-based correction).^15^ However, it is
    generally advised against applying detrending methods *before* the
    isosbestic correction step, as this can interfere with the proper
    scaling and fitting of the control channel.^15^ This suggests a
    hierarchical approach: primary correction via the isosbestic fit,
    followed by a potential secondary detrending of the resultant signal
    if slow, non-artifactual drifts persist or if the initial correction
    was incomplete.\
    **B. Motion Artifact Correction: Leveraging the Isosbestic
    Control**\
    Motion artifacts are a significant source of contamination in fiber
    photometry recordings from freely moving animals. These artifacts
    arise from physical movements of the animal, which can cause bending
    of the fiber optic cable, slight shifts in the implant position
    relative to the targeted brain tissue, or changes in light coupling
    efficiency. Such events lead to fluctuations in the detected
    fluorescence intensity that are unrelated to neural activity.\
    **1. Principles of Isosbestic Correction**\
    The cornerstone of motion artifact correction in modern fiber
    photometry is the use of an isosbestic control channel.^7^ For green
    fluorescent sensors like GACh3.0 (which is excited by blue light,
    typically \~465-470 nm), the isosbestic excitation wavelength is
    usually around 405-415 nm (violet light). At this isosbestic point,
    the fluorescence emission of the sensor is largely independent of
    its binding to the target ligand (in this case, ACh).^7^ Therefore,
    fluctuations observed in the fluorescence signal elicited by
    isosbestic excitation are presumed to reflect non-specific changes
    common to both the signal and control channels. These include
    artifacts due to motion, fiber bending, hemodynamic changes
    (variations in blood flow or oxygenation that alter light
    absorption/scattering), and, as discussed earlier,
    photobleaching.^7^ The assumption is that these artifacts affect
    both the signal and control channels in a proportional manner.\
    **2. Fitting the Control Channel (e.g., 405nm) to the Signal Channel
    (e.g., 465nm for GACh3.0)**\
    To remove motion artifacts, the control channel (405 nm) is
    mathematically fitted to the signal channel (465 nm). The fitted
    control trace then serves as an estimate of the artifactual
    component within the signal channel.

**3. Sufficiency of Isosbestic Correction vs. Need for Additional
Detrending**\
While the fitting of an isosbestic control signal is fundamental for
correcting acute motion artifacts and the primary component of
photobleaching, it may not always remove all sources of baseline drift
or very slow signal fluctuations.^15^ Some of these slow drifts might
have a biological origin (e.g., extremely slow, tonic shifts in
acetylcholine levels that are not the primary focus of an event-related
analysis) or might arise from instrumental factors not perfectly
correlated between the two channels over very long timescales.\
Therefore, even after robust isosbestic correction and subsequent ΔF/F
calculation, it may sometimes be necessary to apply an additional
detrending step to the *corrected* signal. This is particularly relevant
when analyzing phasic, event-related neural signals against a baseline
that still exhibits some slow, residual drift. If such additional
detrending is deemed necessary, methods like applying a smoothed moving
median are generally recommended over a simple moving mean. A moving
median is less likely to be influenced by, and thus inadvertently
remove, the actual phasic neural signals of interest, a problem to which
a moving mean can be susceptible (analogous to the outlier sensitivity
of OLS regression).^15^\
**C. Baseline (F0) Estimation for Normalization**\
1. Importance of F0\
The baseline fluorescence, denoted as F0, is a critical reference point
in fiber photometry analysis. Normalization methods, particularly the
widely used ΔF/F calculation, express the change in fluorescence (ΔF) as
a fraction of this baseline level. Therefore, an accurate and
appropriate estimation of F0 is essential for obtaining reliable and
interpretable normalized signals. Errors or biases in F0 estimation will
directly propagate to the normalized signal, potentially distorting the
perceived magnitude and kinetics of neural responses.\
**2. Methods for F0 Determination**\
Several approaches exist for determining F0, often leveraging the
information from the isosbestic control channel:
 
-   Using the Entire Fitted Isosbestic/Control Trace as F0: This is a
    very common and often robust method. After the control channel
    (e.g., 405 nm) has been fitted to the signal channel (e.g., 465 nm)
    using a regression technique (like OLS or, preferably, IRLS), the
    resulting fitted trace itself is used as the F0 for each time point
    in the recording. The ΔF/F is then calculated as: (Signalraw
    −FittedControl)/FittedControl.^11^ This approach has the advantage
    of providing a dynamic F0 that continuously accounts for
    photobleaching and other slow artifacts that are captured by the
    control channel and reflected in the fit. The GuPPy analysis
    toolkit, for example, employs this method.^23^
 
-   Averaging a Pre-event or Sliding Baseline Window:
 
    -   Pre-event Baseline: Particularly in event-related experimental
        designs, F0 can be defined as the average fluorescence of the
        signal channel (or, more robustly, the fitted control trace or
        the artifact-corrected signal trace) during a defined period
        immediately preceding each discrete event of interest.^14^ For
        instance, FiPhA describes normalization relative to a baseline
        reference interval \'r\', where the mean of this interval (r̄)
        serves as F0.^12^ This method localizes the F0 estimation to the
        period just before a potential stimulus-evoked change, making it
        specific to the conditions immediately preceding that event.
 
    -   Sliding Window: A moving average or moving median can be
        calculated over a sliding window of the signal trace (or the
        fitted control trace) to generate a continuously adapting F0.
        The size of this window is a critical parameter; it must be long
        enough to smooth out rapid neural transients but short enough to
        adapt to slower, genuine baseline shifts.\
        **3. Implications of Different F0 Selection Strategies**\
        The choice of F0 estimation strategy can have significant
        implications for the final normalized signal:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Entire Fitted Isosbestic Trace as F0: This method offers continuous
    correction for artifacts like photobleaching that are
    well-represented in the control channel\'s fit. It is generally
    suitable for analyzing phasic responses against a slowly changing
    baseline. However, a potential pitfall is that if the regression fit
    is imperfect, or if there are very slow, genuine neural signals that
    happen to correlate with some component of the control channel\'s
    activity (even if not causally related), these neural signals might
    be partially regressed out or incorporated into the F0, thereby
    diminishing their apparent magnitude in the ΔF/F trace.
 
-   Pre-event Baseline Average: This approach is often more robust to
    very slow, sustained changes in neural activity that occur over the
    course of an entire recording session, as it defines the baseline
    locally for each event. This can prevent such long-term tonic shifts
    from being misinterpreted as baseline drift by a globally defined
    F0. However, this method assumes that the chosen pre-event period is
    indeed a true, quiescent baseline, free of anticipatory activity or
    other confounding signals. It can also be sensitive to noise or
    random fluctuations occurring within that specific pre-event
    window.\
    The optimal strategy for F0 selection can depend on the specific
    characteristics of the neural signal being investigated (e.g., rapid
    phasic responses versus slower tonic changes) and the overall
    duration of the recording. For analyzing rapid, event-locked phasic
    signals, using a pre-event baseline for F0 calculation can be highly
    appropriate, as it tailors the baseline to the immediate temporal
    context of the event. For longer recordings where the primary
    interest lies in tracking overall activity patterns or phasic events
    occurring against a backdrop of slow drifts (like photobleaching),
    using the entire fitted control trace as a dynamic F0 is a common
    and effective practice for continuous artifact correction. The
    decision should be guided by the experimental question and the
    expected temporal dynamics of the ACh signal.\
    **D. Signal Normalization: ΔF/F and Z-score**\
    Once artifacts have been addressed and a baseline (F0) has been
    estimated, the fluorescence signal is typically normalized.
    Normalization serves to account for variations in absolute
    fluorescence intensity that can arise from differences in sensor
    expression levels, implant depth or quality, light source power, and
    detector sensitivity across animals, sessions, or even within a
    single long recording (due to photobleaching if not perfectly
    corrected by F0). The two most common normalization methods are ΔF/F
    and z-scoring.\
    **1. ΔF/F Calculation**\
    ΔF/F represents the change in fluorescence (ΔF=F−F0) relative to the
    baseline fluorescence (F0), and is often expressed as a percentage
    change.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Common Formulas:
 
    -   The general conceptual formula is ΔF/F=(F−F0)/F0.
 
    -   When an isosbestic control channel is used, and its fitted
        version (FittedControl) is taken as F0, a robust and widely
        adopted formula, notably advocated by Lerner et al. (2015) and
        implemented in tools like GuPPy, is: ΔF/F=(Signalraw
        −FittedControl)/FittedControl.^11^ This formula effectively uses
        the fitted control trace as a dynamic baseline that corrects for
        shared artifacts.
 
    -   The FiPhA toolkit calculates ΔF/F as (xi −rˉ)/rˉ, where xi is
        the current signal value and r̄ is the mean of a user-defined
        reference (baseline) interval.^12^\
        **2. Z-score Calculation**\
        The z-score transforms the signal by expressing each data point
        in terms of its deviation from a mean, scaled by the standard
        deviation. This represents the change in units of standard
        deviation.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Standard Z-score: Calculated as z=(X−μ)/σ, where X is the signal
    value (often the already artifact-corrected or ΔF/F signal), and μ
    and σ are the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of either
    the entire recording trace or a defined baseline period.^14^
 
-   Baseline-Referenced Z-score: In this variation, μ and σ are
    specifically calculated from a designated baseline period (e.g., a
    pre-event window). GuPPy offers this option.^23^
 
-   Robust/Modified Z-score: To reduce sensitivity to outliers that can
    affect the mean and standard deviation, a robust z-score can be
    calculated using the median and the Median Absolute Deviation (MAD)
    instead. FiPhA implements a robust z-score as RZi (x)=(%ΔF/Fi
    (x)−median(%ΔF/F(r)))/MAD(%ΔF/F(r)).^14^ GuPPy also offers a
    modified z-score using median and MAD.^23^\

**E. Considerations Specific to GACh3.0 Sensor Characteristics**\
The specific properties of the GACh3.0 sensor itself should inform both
data acquisition strategies and analytical choices to ensure that the
recorded signals accurately reflect underlying cholinergic dynamics.\
**1. GACh3.0 Kinetics**
 
-   EC50 (Half-maximal Effective Concentration): The EC50 of GACh3.0 in
    HEK293T cells is reported to be approximately 2.2 µM.^35^ This value
    indicates the concentration of ACh at which the sensor exhibits half
    of its maximal fluorescence response, providing insight into the
    physiological range of ACh concentrations the sensor is most
    sensitive to.
 
-   Response Speed: GACh3.0 has been demonstrated to be capable of
    measuring rapid decreases in task-evoked ACh levels, with responses
    observed within 250 ms.^4^ Its predecessor, GACh2.0, had reported
    kinetics of approximately 200 ms for rise time (τrise ) and 700 ms
    for decay time (τdecay ).^2^ Although specific on-off rates for
    GACh3.0 are not extensively detailed in the provided material, its
    characterization as an \"optimized\" sensor suggests it is
    well-suited for tracking physiologically relevant ACh transients,
    including relatively fast events.\
    **2. Impact on Acquisition Parameters**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Sampling Rate: The data acquisition sampling rate must be adequate
    to capture the fastest expected dynamics of ACh release and reuptake
    as reported by GACh3.0. Given that the sensor can respond within
    \~200-250 ms, the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem dictates that the
    sampling rate should be at least twice the highest frequency
    component of interest. To faithfully capture such rapid events, a
    minimal sampling rate would be above 8-10 Hz. However, to achieve
    better temporal resolution and more accurately define the shape of
    these transients, higher sampling rates are generally preferred.
    Rates in the range of 20-100 Hz are commonly used in fiber
    photometry ^37^, though one source notes that for millisecond-level
    calcium changes, extremely high acquisition frequencies might be
    unnecessary.^37^ For GACh3.0, a sampling rate between 20 Hz and 50
    Hz likely represents a good balance between temporal fidelity and
    data volume. It is noted that for a red-shifted ACh sensor (rACh1h),
    which is reported to have faster kinetics than GACh3.0, data
    acquisition at 10 Hz was used in one study ^35^, suggesting that
    even 10 Hz might be considered adequate by some for certain ACh
    sensors, though higher rates are generally safer for capturing fast
    transients.\
    **3. Impact on Analysis Choices**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Filter Parameters: The choice of low-pass filter cutoff frequency is
    directly influenced by the sensor\'s kinetics. To preserve true,
    fast ACh transients (e.g., those occurring within 200-300 ms), the
    cutoff frequency should be set sufficiently high (e.g., typically
    \>2-4 Hz, and potentially higher depending on the precise speed of
    the fastest relevant physiological event and the desired temporal
    resolution) while still effectively removing higher frequency
    noise.^11^ Applying a filter with a very low cutoff (e.g., 1 Hz)
    could artificially slow down, diminish, or even obliterate these
    rapid physiological changes, leading to an inaccurate representation
    of ACh dynamics.
 
-   Baseline Window Selection: When performing event-related analyses,
    the time window selected for F0 calculation (if using a pre-event
    baseline method) must be carefully chosen to avoid encroaching on
    the earliest phase of the ACh response. If the baseline window
    inadvertently includes part of the rising or falling phase of an ACh
    transient, the F0 estimate will be inaccurate, consequently
    distorting the calculated ΔF/F.\
    The known kinetic properties of the GACh3.0 sensor should serve as a
    direct guide for selecting appropriate data acquisition and analysis
    parameters. For example, if the research question involves analyzing
    very rapid, cue-induced dips in ACh concentration, the entire
    processing pipeline---from sampling rate during acquisition to the
    low-pass filter cutoff and the definition of baseline windows for F0
    estimation---must be optimized to preserve these fast temporal
    features. Failure to do so, for instance by over-filtering the data
    or using an inappropriately placed baseline window, could lead to a
    significant underestimation or mischaracterization of the true speed
    and magnitude of cholinergic signaling events. This underscores the
    need for a holistic approach where sensor characteristics inform
    every step of the data acquisition and analysis workflow.\
    **IV. Recommended Analysis Pipelines and Available Tools**\
    A variety of software tools and analysis pipelines are available to
    researchers working with fiber photometry data. These range from
    comprehensive open-source toolkits with graphical user interfaces
    (GUIs) to vendor-specific software development kits (SDKs) that
    allow for more customized scripting.\
    **A. Overview of Established Open-Source Toolkits and Vendor
    Resources**\
    1. GuPPy (Guided Photometry Analysis in Python)\
    GuPPy is a free, open-source tool developed in Python and presented
    as a Jupyter notebook, designed to be accessible for users with
    minimal programming experience through its GUIs.^18^
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Key Features: GuPPy handles isosbestic correction by fitting the
    control channel to the signal channel using a least-squares
    polynomial fit. If an isosbestic channel is not available or not
    used, it can generate a control trace by applying an exponential fit
    to the signal channel to approximate photobleaching.^23^ It
    calculates ΔF/F using the formula:
    (Signal−FittedControl)/FittedControl. GuPPy also offers options for
    z-score normalization, including standard z-score,
    baseline-referenced z-score, and a modified z-score using median and
    MAD. For signal smoothing, it applies a moving average filter. Peak
    detection is implemented based on thresholding the z-score trace
    using the median absolute deviation within a moving window.^23^\
    **2. FiPhA (Fiber Photometry Analysis)**\
    FiPhA is a general-purpose fiber photometry analysis application
    built as an R Shiny app, making it highly interactive and
    user-friendly.^12^ It supports data import from a wide range of
    photometry systems and formats, including TDT, Plexon, and
    spectrally resolved systems.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Key Features: FiPhA offers functionalities such as linear unmixing
    for spectrally resolved data, photobleaching correction using linear
    or exponential models, and various normalization options including
    ΔF/F, standard z-score, and robust z-score.^12^ For ΔF/F
    calculation, F0 is typically determined from the mean of a
    user-defined reference (baseline) interval. It also provides tools
    for event-triggered averaging and filtering of behavioral
    events.^12^\
    **3. TMAC (Two-channel Motion Artifact Correction)**\
    TMAC is not a standalone GUI toolkit but rather a computational
    method based on a generative statistical model and Bayesian
    inference, designed to separate neural activity, motion artifact,
    and noise from two-channel imaging data.^30^
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Key Features: Its strength lies in its model-based approach to
    artifact correction, which may offer advantages over simpler
    regression methods, especially when channel-independent noise is
    significant. Implementation of TMAC would typically require custom
    scripting or integration into a broader analysis pipeline.\
    **4. Vendor-Specific Tools/Examples**
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT): TDT provides robust support for
    data analysis through its MATLAB and Python Software Development
    Kits (SDKs). Key functions include TDTbin2mat for importing data
    into MATLAB and the tdt Python package for Python-based
    analysis.^41^ TDT also offers example scripts and workbooks for
    common analysis tasks, such as peri-event epoch averaging and
    fitting the 405 nm control channel to the 465 nm signal channel for
    detrending/artifact correction.^10^ Their Synapse software is used
    for data acquisition, and Pynapse can be used for integrating
    behavioral control and performing some online data processing.^41^
 
-   Plexon: Plexon\'s Multi-Wavelength Photometry System comes with
    software that allows visualization of raw data and heatmaps. The
    system is designed to interleave excitation for the signal
    wavelength (e.g., 465 nm) and the isosbestic control wavelength
    (e.g., 410 nm), facilitating artifact correction.^44^\

```{=html}

```
-   pMAT and pyPhotometry: These are mentioned as other available

-   PhAT (Photometry Analysis Toolkit): This is another GUI-based,
    Python-driven toolkit designed for flexibility. It supports data
    import from various sources, including Neurophotometrics systems and

-   GitHub Repositories: Several publicly available GitHub repositories,

    Tom-top/fiber_photometry_analysis ^48^, provide Python scripts
    tailored for fiber photometry analysis, often for data acquired with
    TDT systems. These scripts typically include steps for data
    smoothing, baseline correction (addressing signal decay and
    bleaching), normalization (often via standardization, resulting in a
    z-score-like output, or ΔF/F), and regression between the signal and
    control channels to derive a corrected signal (sometimes referred to
    as zdF/F or dF/F).^48^\
    **B. Guidance on Selecting or Customizing an Analysis Pipeline**\
    The choice of an appropriate analysis pipeline or tool depends on
    several factors:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Data Acquisition System: Some tools are optimized for data from
    specific vendors (e.g., GuPPy was initially developed for TDT data,
    though expanded ^18^).
 
-   Experimental Design: The nature of the experiment (e.g., discrete
    event-related responses versus analysis of spontaneous activity or
    long-term tonic changes) will influence the suitability of different
    normalization and F0 estimation strategies.
 
-   User\'s Programming Proficiency: GUI-based tools like GuPPy, FiPhA,

    coding experience, as they provide a structured workflow and visual
    feedback.^12^
 
-   Specific Analytical Needs: For highly customized analyses, advanced
    statistical modeling, or integration with unique behavioral

    custom-written code will likely be necessary.\
    It is important to recognize that the proliferation of analysis
    tools and pipelines underscores the fact that there is no single
    \"one-size-fits-all\" solution for every fiber photometry
    experiment. Researchers must develop a clear understanding of the
    underlying assumptions and mathematical operations performed by any
    chosen software package or script. Blindly applying a tool without
    this understanding can lead to erroneous conclusions. The ability to
    critically evaluate the appropriateness of a tool for specific data
    characteristics (e.g., sensor type, noise profile) and experimental
    questions, and ideally, the capacity to customize or at least
    thoroughly comprehend the core algorithms, are crucial for ensuring
    the scientific rigor of the findings.\
    **V. Advanced Considerations and Future Perspectives**\
    The field of fiber photometry is continually evolving, with ongoing
    advancements in sensor technology, data acquisition methods, and
    analytical approaches. These developments aim to enhance the
    precision, reliability, and depth of information that can be
    extracted from *in vivo* optical recordings.\
    **A. Analysis of Multi-Channel Recordings**\
    The capability to simultaneously monitor multiple neurochemical
    signals or to correlate neurotransmitter dynamics with the activity
    of specific neural populations is a powerful extension of fiber
    photometry. This often involves co-expressing GACh3.0 with other
    sensors, such as GCaMP for calcium-dependent neural activity, or
    sensors for other neurotransmitters like dopamine or serotonin.^25^
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Technical Challenges: Multi-channel recordings necessitate careful
    management of spectral crosstalk between different fluorophores.
    This involves selecting sensors with well-separated excitation and
    emission spectra, using appropriate filter sets, and potentially
    employing spectral unmixing algorithms if significant overlap
    exists.^35^
 
-   Analytical Approaches: Data from each channel must typically be
    processed independently through artifact correction and
    normalization steps. Subsequent analysis then focuses on examining
    the temporal relationships, correlations, or potential causal
    interactions between the different recorded signals. Advanced
    time-series analysis techniques may be employed to understand these
    interdependencies.\
    **B. Emerging Techniques**\
    Several innovative techniques are emerging that promise to overcome
    some of the limitations of traditional intensity-based fiber
    photometry:
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Fluorescence Lifetime Photometry: This approach measures changes in
    the fluorescence lifetime of the sensor rather than just its
    intensity. Fluorescence lifetime can be an intrinsic property of the
    sensor\'s state (e.g., bound vs. unbound to its ligand) and is often
    less susceptible to variations in sensor expression levels,
    excitation light power fluctuations, or some forms of
    photobleaching.^49^ The GRAB-ACh3.0 sensor has been shown to exhibit
    a significant change in fluorescence lifetime upon ACh binding,
    making it a candidate for this technique.^49^ Lifetime-based
    measurements could potentially offer more robust and quantitative
    comparisons of ACh dynamics across different animals, brain regions,
    or longitudinal recording sessions, circumventing some of the
    challenges associated with F0 stability and intensity normalization.
 
-   Functional Linear Mixed Modeling (FLMM): Traditional fiber
    photometry analysis often involves averaging signals across many
    trials or time-locking signals to discrete events. While useful,
    such averaging can obscure important trial-to-trial variability or
    subtle temporal dynamics. FLMM has been proposed as a more advanced
    statistical framework for analyzing fiber photometry data at the
    trial level.^19^ This approach can model the entire temporal profile
    of the response on each trial while accounting for various sources
    of variability (e.g., across subjects, sessions, or trials),
    potentially offering greater statistical power and more nuanced
    insights into how experimental variables affect neural signals over
    time.^19^\
    **C. The Push Towards Standardized Reporting and Analysis**\
    There is a growing recognition within the neuroscience community of
    the critical need for greater standardization in the analysis of
    fiber photometry data and more transparent reporting of
    methodological details.^6^ This movement is driven by the desire to
    improve the reproducibility and comparability of findings across
    different laboratories.
 
```{=html}
<!-- -->
```
-   Key Elements: Efforts towards standardization include the
    development and dissemination of open-source analysis tools with
    clear documentation, the establishment of best-practice guidelines
    for data processing and artifact correction, and a community-wide
    emphasis on detailed and unambiguous reporting of all analytical
    parameters in publications.\
    The trajectory of the field indicates a clear movement towards more
    sophisticated analytical and statistical methodologies. These
    advanced approaches are designed to handle the inherent
    trial-by-trial variability in biological signals more effectively
    and to provide richer, more nuanced insights than can be obtained
    from simple peri-event averages or basic normalization techniques.
    The development and adoption of techniques like FLMM and
    fluorescence lifetime photometry are indicative of this shift,
    promising a future where fiber photometry analysis becomes
    increasingly quantitative, statistically rigorous, and capable of
    extracting deeper understanding from complex *in vivo* datasets.\
    **VI. Summary and Expert Recommendations**\
    The analysis of GACh3.0 fiber photometry data requires a meticulous,
    multi-step approach to transform raw fluorescence signals into
    reliable indicators of *in vivo* acetylcholine dynamics. The
    accuracy and interpretability of the final results are critically
    dependent on the appropriate application of methods for artifact
    correction, baseline estimation, and signal normalization.\
    **A. Recap of Critical Processing Steps for GACh3.0 Fiber
    Photometry**
 
1.  Data Acquisition & Quality Control: Ensure high-quality raw data
    acquisition with simultaneous recording of the GACh3.0 signal and an
    isosbestic control signal. Perform initial visual inspection to
    exclude segments with gross artifacts.
 
2.  Low-Pass Filtering: Apply a zero-phase low-pass filter with a cutoff
    frequency carefully chosen to remove high-frequency noise without
    attenuating the true, potentially rapid, kinetics of GACh3.0
    responses.
 
3.  Motion Artifact & Photobleaching Correction: Utilize the isosbestic
    control channel. Fit the control channel to the signal channel,
    preferably using Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS)
    regression, to generate a model of shared artifacts.
 
4.  F0 Estimation: Determine the baseline fluorescence (F0). A common
    and robust method is to use the fitted isosbestic trace as a dynamic
    F0. Alternatively, for event-related analyses, the average of a
    pre-event period of the corrected signal or fitted isosbestic trace
    can be used.
 
5.  Normalization: Calculate ΔF/F using the formula (Signalraw
    −F0fitted_isosbestic )/F0fitted_isosbestic . While z-scoring is an
    option, it should be used with extreme caution for GACh3.0 data,
    particularly for quantifying spontaneous activity or comparing
    conditions that may alter baseline signal variance, due to its
    potential to distort true signal changes.
 
6.  Further Detrending (if necessary): For the corrected and normalized
    signal, additional detrending (e.g., using a moving median) may be
    applied if slow, residual drifts unrelated to the signals of
    interest persist, especially in long recordings.\
